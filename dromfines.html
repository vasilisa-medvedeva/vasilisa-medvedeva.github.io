Case 01

Trust-Building Onboarding

Problem

A significant share of new users never completed registration: they opened the app once and didn’t return.

Users were asked to provide documents before they understood why the app was valuable or why they should trust it with their data.

Solution

My hypothesis was that the drop-off was caused by a lack of trust, not lack of interest.

I designed a short trust-building onboarding with animated Lottie screens that addressed two key concerns: the data comes from official sources (GIBDD, Federal Treasury, MADI, AMPP, and FSSP), and checking a car only requires one scan of the registration certificate.

We validated the solution through a month-long A/B test against the original flow without onboarding.

Outcome

  • The onboarding variant increased registrations by 16%
Case 02

Registration Flow Optimization

Problem

The app could already scan documents instead of asking users to type the plate number and registration certificate details, but most users never found that option — scanning was hidden behind a small camera icon inside the input field.

As a result, users defaulted to the slowest path right on the product’s very first screen.

The input fields themselves looked dated too: thin underlines in place of full fields gave almost no feedback as you typed.

Solution

I reworked the screen’s hierarchy: scanning became the primary action at the top of the screen, with manual entry as the fallback below it.

For scanning, we used the system document-recognition pattern (VisionKit), which gave reliable recognition of plates and registration certificates without building a custom solution from scratch.

I also updated the form: replaced the dated underline fields with modern outlined fields, persistent labels, and clear focus states.

The new screen structure also accounted for a future registration scenario via driver’s license.

Outcome

  • Registration drop-off fell by 20%
  • Scanning became the first and most visible path for new users
  • Manual entry remained a fallback option
  • The same design later let us add driver’s license registration without reworking the screen
Case 03

Reducing Friction in Payments

Problem

Every payment started from scratch: the app couldn’t save a card, and Apple Pay and Google Pay weren’t supported.

Users had to re-enter card details every time they paid a fine. Repeat users — the core audience for this business-critical flow — felt it most.

Solution

I redesigned the payment screen around saved payment methods: instead of re-entering card details, the user could simply confirm the payment.

The chosen payment method was remembered for future use, so the next fine payment opened already to be confirmed.

Before release, we validated the new flow through usability testing to make sure it was clear to users.

Outcome

  • Payment conversion doubled
  • Repeat payments went from re-entering card details to confirming a single saved payment method
Case 04

Clarifying Fine Details

Problem

The fine screen was a long list of fields with no visual hierarchy: date, amount, photo, violation location, document number, and the issuing authority.

Meanwhile, information about the 50% early-payment discount — the key factor in deciding whether to pay right away — wasn’t highlighted at all. The screen also didn’t explain what the fine was actually for.

Solution

I restructured the screen, grouping information into logical sections: time and place of the violation, the article, the photo, and the ruling.

I moved the discount information into a separate card at the top of the screen and added a clear deadline for it.

I moved the violation photo lower — it took longer to load, so users would see the key information first without waiting.

An “Article” block also appeared, describing the violation, so users understand the reason for the fine instead of seeing just the law’s article number.

Outcome

  • The discount information and its deadline became the most visible element on the screen
  • Users immediately understood what they were fined for, not just how much to pay
  • The long list became a clear structure with a defined visual hierarchy
